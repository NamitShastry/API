Document Purpose and How to Read This
This document has two jobs, stitched into one continuous narrative.
Job one is venture architecture: an honest, numbers-first stress test of whether the SIH26056
problem statement — Real-Time Airfare Price Index (APIx) for India — can be turned, after the
hackathon, into a real company with a credible path to roughly ₹100 crore in annual revenue
within 30 months of commercial launch. This is Part I.
Job two is a complete product and engineering specification detailed enough to build from
— the SIH submission, the commercial platform it grows into, and everything in between: in-
formation architecture, every dashboard, the login and security model, the full SQL schema,
the real-time engine, the API contract, and the pricing and go-to-market plan. This is Parts II
through IV.
The two jobs are deliberately fused, because the strongest SIH submissions are not toy
hackathon demos — they are the first slice of a real company, built with the discipline of a
company from day one. Judges notice this. So do the first paying customers, eighteen months
later.
A note on intellectual honesty, because this document follows a strict rule throughout: every
revenue number is shown with its underlying math, and every aggressive assump-
tion is flagged as an assumption, not smuggled in as a fact. Where the ₹100cr-in-30-
months target is genuinely difficult to hit on this specific wedge, that is stated plainly, along
with what would have to be true for it to work, and what the realistic base case looks like
instead.

PART I — VENTURE ARCHITECTURE
1. Executive Thesis
What the company is.
AeroIndex (working name; commercial name proposed later as
FareOS) starts as a hackathon submission that automatically tracks Indian domestic airfares
across airlines and OTAs and turns them into a real-time, MoSPI-methodology-aligned price in-
dex — the Airfare Price Index (APIx). It becomes a company by generalizing that same pipeline
— fare collection, data-quality engineering, index construction, and dashboarding — into In-
dia’s fare-intelligence and travel-price-data infrastructure layer, sold three ways at
once:
1. To government and public institutions (MoSPI/NSO, RBI, DGCA, state tourism boards)
as an official or semi-official statistical data product and dashboard — the anchor,
credibility-conferring customer.
2. To commercial buyers of fare intelligence — corporate travel management compa-
nies, OTAs, airlines’ revenue-management teams, fintech/expense platforms, and market-
research/equity-research desks — as a B2B data and analytics subscription.
3. To consumers indirectly, via a free public index (press citations, SEO, trust) that feeds
distribution into the paid tiers, in the same way Hopper’s public price-prediction feature
fed its fintech attach-rate business.
The one-line thesis: India does not have an independent, high-frequency, transparent mea-
sure of what people actually pay for domestic air travel — the same infrastructure gap that
FRED (US) fills for macro data and that ATPCO/OAG/Cirium fill for global airline distribution —
and government, corporates and financial markets will all pay for a trustworthy version of it
once it exists.
The honest caveat, stated up front and expanded fully in §19–20: the ₹100cr-in-30-
months target is achievable only through the commercial B2B/enterprise tracks, not
through the government contract alone, and it requires successful execution on at least
two of the three buyer segments simultaneously, plus at least one funding round. The base
case, absent funding and absent multi-segment execution, plateaus far below ₹100cr in that
window. This is not a reason to abandon the idea — it is a reason to build the commercial tracks
in parallel with the hackathon from week one, not as an afterthought.

2.2 Frequency, urgency, and growth
Airfare volatility is not a one-time event — every day, on every route, is a new data point, which
is exactly what makes this a subscription-shaped, not a project-shaped, problem. The pain
also grows over time: as more of India’s ~150–160 million annual domestic air passengers
book digitally, and as dynamic pricing gets more algorithmically aggressive (more OTAs, more
fare families, more personalization), the gap between “what CPI captures” and “what people
actually pay” widens, not narrows. This is a problem that gets more valuable to solve
every year, which is the trait Part I §20 asks every candidate opportunity to have.
2.3 Painkiller or nice-to-have?
• For MoSPI/RBI: closer to mission-critical infrastructure gap, but the buying process
is slow, political, and budget-constrained — a classic government-sales paradox (high
importance, low urgency-to-pay).
• For corporate travel managers: painkiller once framed as “you are provably overpaying,”
but requires proof, not a promise, to convert into a paid contract.
• For OTA/airline revenue management: nice-to-have-that-becomes-a-painkiller the
moment a competitor buys it first — classic B2B data-product dynamics (see ATPCO’s
own growth pattern in §13).
• For research/analyst desks: nice-to-have, low willingness-to-pay individually, but high
brand value and free distribution.
2.4 Who owns the purchasing decision, who benefits, who pays
These are not the same people, which is the single biggest structural risk in the government
track and must be designed around:
• MoSPI: benefits (better CPI), but budget cycles and procurement rules make paying slow
— realistic first engagement is a pilot/MoU/data-sharing agreement, not a purchase
order, in year one.
• RBI: benefits, rarely pays directly for external data products at small-vendor scale without
an empanelment process.
• Corporate travel managers: benefit and pay, and can sign a subscription in weeks, not
years — this is why the commercial track, not the government track, must carry the ₹100cr
math.

4. India’s corporate-travel and business-travel market is independently forecast

tech buyers in that market are increasingly used to paying for SaaS dashboards (ITILITE’s
growth is proof), so the commercial buyer’s willingness to pay for a travel-cost-intelligence
layer is already being trained by adjacent products.
5. The “price-prediction as fintech-attach” model has been proven at large
scale by Hopper (~USD 850M revenue in 2024, ~40% of bookings touching a price-
certainty/fintech product), demonstrating that a fare-prediction data asset, monetized
correctly, can support a very large business — validating the category, even though
Hopper is consumer-first and AeroIndex is infrastructure/B2B-first.
Why hasn’t someone already captured this? Because the pieces sit at an unusual inter-
section that most single-type companies don’t span: global data vendors (ATPCO, OAG, Cirium)
build for airline/GDS-scale schedule and fare-filing data, not India-specific retail-price indices
with CPI-grade statistical rigor; Indian OTAs (MakeMyTrip, ixigo, Cleartrix, Cleartrip) have the
fare data as a byproduct but monetize it only internally, with zero incentive to sell it externally
as an index (it would arm competitors and regulators simultaneously); and government statis-
tical agencies have the mandate but not the modern data-engineering execution speed. The
wedge is precisely the gap between “who has the mandate,” “who has the data,”
and “who has the execution speed” — no single incumbent currently owns all three.

distribution plans are the most common way promising B2B ideas die quietly.
Track A — Government credibility anchor (not counted in the ₹100cr math, but re-

• Exact target: the MoSPI Price Statistics Division team and DGCA data-policy contacts
who authored/sponsored SIH26056, plus the SIH mentor(s) assigned to problem statement

dependently produced an index tracking within X% of DGCA’s published averages over a

14. Data Compounding — does the flywheel actually exist?
Yes, but it is a trust flywheel more than a classic ML data-network-effect flywheel,
and the distinction matters:
More days of collection →richer historical baseline & better back-tested accu-
racy →more citable/creditable public index →more press/analyst citations and
MoSPI trust →easier enterprise and government sales →more paying logos →more
budget for wider route coverage and faster refresh cycles →richer data →repeat
This is not a flywheel where each customer’s own usage directly improves the product for other
customers (there is no equivalent of “your bookings train our model to be better for everyone”
the way a true network-effect data business works) — it is closer to how a credit bureau or
a benchmark-index provider (e.g., a CPI itself, or a bond-index provider) compounds: time
and coverage are the moat, not per-customer data contribution. This should be stated
exactly this way to investors — overclaiming a network effect that doesn’t structurally exist is
the kind of assumption an adversarial investor will find in minutes (§20).

46. Deeper Competitor & Comparable Case Studies
46.1 Hopper — the clearest proof that price-prediction data monetizes at scale
Hopper’s public numbers (Business of Apps, 2026 compilation) show roughly USD 850 mil-
lion in 2024 revenue, up 21.4% year over year, built on a proprietary cost-prediction engine
analyzing tens of billions of price points, with fintech attach products (Price Freeze, Can-
cel/Change for Any Reason, Flight Disruption Guarantee) driving roughly 40% of
2024 bookings and over 60% of users engaging at least one such feature in 2022. What
this proves for AeroIndex: a fare-prediction data asset, monetized correctly, supports a
very large business. What it does not prove: that the same consumer-fintech-attach model
transplants easily into India — Hopper took roughly a decade, hundreds of millions in ven-
ture funding, and a US/Canada-first consumer-app distribution advantage to get there; AeroIn-
dex’s B2B/government-first strategy in this document is deliberately a different, more capital-
efficient path suited to a founder without that funding base, not an attempt to replicate Hop-
per’s playbook directly.
46.2 ATPCO — the airline-owned infrastructure model
ATPCO is owned cooperatively by the airlines whose fares it files and distributes, giving it struc-
tural trust and data completeness no outside vendor could easily replicate — its business is
fare-filing and rules infrastructure for GDSs and OTAs (Amadeus, Sabre, Expedia Group, and
newer AI-pricing players like Fetcherr are named partners on ATPCO’s own materials), sold
via enterprise, custom-priced subscriptions and shopping/pricing APIs. What this proves for
AeroIndex: airline-grade fare-data infrastructure is a real, durable, enterprise-priced cate-
gory. The structural difference: ATPCO’s moat is ownership (the airlines themselves are
the shareholders); AeroIndex cannot replicate that ownership structure and must instead build
its moat from independence and government-adjacent trust (Part I §13) — a different but real
basis for durability, closer to how an independent ratings agency is trusted precisely because
it isn’t owned by the entities it rates.
46.3 OAG / Cirium — global schedule and status data, not retail-price elasticity
Both are established, enterprise-trusted providers of flight schedule, status, and (Cirium in par-
ticular) some market-data products to airlines, airports, and aerospace/finance clients — deep
historical datasets, global coverage, decades of trust. The gap they leave open: neither
is built around India-specific, lead-time-resolved, CPI-methodology-aligned retail fare indices
— their businesses are structured around schedule/operational data and industry-wide market
analytics, not the specific “what does a traveler actually pay, at what lead time, on this spe-
cific India route” question AeroIndex answers. This is a genuine, specific, defensible gap, not
a claim that AeroIndex could out-compete either company on their actual core business.
46.4 ITILITE and Indian corporate-travel SaaS — validates the buyer, not a direct
competitor
ITILITE (backed by investors including Matrix Partners India) built a corporate travel-and-
expense management SaaS business serving Indian enterprises — direct evidence that Indian
corporate travel buyers already pay for SaaS dashboards layered on top of their travel spend.
Critically, ITILITE’s own product optimizes booking and policy workflow within its
own inventory and integrations — it has no structural incentive to independently
benchmark whether its own negotiated fares are actually competitive against the
broader market, which is precisely the neutral-benchmark gap AeroIndex’s Corporate/TMC
track (Part I §9, Track B) is positioned to fill, ideally as a complement sitting alongside a TMC
like ITILITE rather than a head-on competitor to its booking workflow.

57. Full Public Methodology Page — Sample Text (/index/methodology)
The following is the actual copy proposed for the public-facing methodology page, since trans-
parency of exactly this kind is the core trust asset for both the government and enterprise
tracks (Part I §13–14).
How the AeroIndex APIx is built
1. What we measure. AeroIndex tracks a locked basket of 20 directional domestic
routes, selected by ranking city pairs on DGCA passenger-traffic data and requiring at
least three independent sources to sell each route. The current basket, its effective
date, and its underlying weight source document are always shown below this page,
never only in a PDF you have to request.
2. How often we observe prices. Fares are observed up to four times per day
(00:30, 06:30, 12:30, 18:30 IST) across seven standard booking-lead-time windows
(1, 3, 7, 15, 30, 45, and 60 days before departure).
3. How we clean the data. Every observed price passes through twelve explicit,
published rules (R01–R12; see the full catalogue linked here) covering schema vali-
dation, currency and component checks, statistical outlier detection, deduplication,
sold-out handling, and temporal consistency. Rejected and flagged items are logged,
and a human data steward reviews anything ambiguous before it can affect the pub-
lished index — nothing is silently discarded without a record.
4. How we compute the index. Within each route/lead-time cell, we compute
an unweighted geometric-mean (Jevons) index of matched-product price relatives
against a weekday-matched base price. Cells are combined into a route-level index
using published lead-time weights, and routes are combined into the headline APIx
using DGCA passenger-traffic-based weights. The exact formulas are published in
full (see Appendix C of the full specification).
5. FLASH vs. OFFICIAL. Throughout the day, you’ll see a provisional “FLASH” value
that updates within seconds of new data. Once a day, at 23:30 IST, we recompute
the full day from durable, audited data and publish the “OFFICIAL” value — this is
the number we stand behind, and it never changes silently after publication; any
correction happens through a documented, dated revision.
6.
What we don’t do. We do not solve CAPTCHAs, log into any site, or create
bookings anywhere. We respect every source’s robots.txt and rate limits. Where
live collection is not yet legally/ethically cleared for a given source, we use a cali-
brated simulator, and we always label simulated data as simulated — never as a live
observation.
7. How to check our work. Our 30-day back-test against DGCA’s own published
average fares is here [link], updated whenever our methodology changes materially.
If you find a discrepancy, tell us — [contact link].

28. Authentication, Login & Authorization
28.1 Login page specification
• Layout: centered card, AeroIndex wordmark, single email field →“Continue” (progressive
disclosure: password or magic-link field appears after email is submitted, reducing initial

• Auth methods offered, in priority order:
1. Email + password (default for individual analyst accounts).
2. Magic link / one-time passcode via email (reduces password-reuse risk, preferred
default for new signups).
3. SSO via Google Workspace / Microsoft Entra ID (OAuth2/OIDC) — required
for enterprise (Corporate/TMC and OTA/Fintech) tenants at the Enterprise tier, since
IT/security teams at that buyer segment routinely require SSO before approving a
new vendor.
4. Government-tenant path: for GOV_VIEWER accounts, login is provisioned by AeroIn-

• Password policy (when password auth is used): minimum 12 characters, checked
against a breached-password list (e.g., a k-anonymity HaveIBeenPwned-style check) at
signup and on password change — never a simple regex-complexity rule alone, which
produces weak-but-“compliant” passwords.
• Session handling: short-lived access token (JWT, 15 minutes) + longer-lived rotating

31.3 Real-Time Index Engine (FLASH/OFFICIAL) — the system’s most distinctive
piece
This is the part of the architecture that makes AeroIndex feel genuinely “real-time” rather than
“a nightly batch job with a dashboard bolted on,” and it is worth specifying precisely because
it is also the piece most likely to be under-built by a competing hackathon team:
1. State lives in Redis, not the primary database, for the live path:
a per-cell hash
(cell:{date}:{route}:{bucket} holding sum_ln and n) and a per-product hash holding
each product’s latest log-relative price.
2. Every new accepted quote triggers an atomic Lua script that updates the cell’s
running sum, recomputes that cell’s Jevons value (exp(sum_ln/n)), then the route index
(from its 7 cells), then the overall index (from all routes) — a small, fast, in-memory cas-
cade, not a full table re-scan.
3. An IndexTick event is published (throttled to at most 4/second/series) so the frontend
can subscribe (via Server-Sent Events or a short-poll fallback) and show the headline
number visibly ticking, which is a genuinely compelling demo moment for both SIH judges
and enterprise prospects.
4. Every 30 seconds, provisional FLASH rows are persisted to Postgres (apix_flash,
apix_cell_daily with status='FLASH') so a service restart can rebuild state from
durable storage rather than losing the day’s progress.
5. At 23:30 IST daily (or manual APPROVER trigger), the OFFICIAL freeze runs: a
full recomputation from the durable fare_quote_clean table (not from Redis state,
deliberately — this is the correctness check), compared against the last FLASH value
(any drift beyond 0.05 points is logged and investigated), and written as the immutable
apix_cell_daily/apix_series_daily rows with status='OFFICIAL'.
6. Late-arriving data never mutates a frozen day — corrections only ever happen
through an explicit, audited revision process (see the dq_issue/audit-log design in §32),
which is the property that makes the OFFICIAL series usable as an official-adjacent statistic
in the first place.

63. End-to-End Sequence Walkthroughs
63.1 Signup →first dashboard view (Corporate/TMC self-serve pilot)
1. Prospect clicks “Start a pilot” on the pricing page (§37.1); lands on a short qualification
form (company name, employee count, primary travel routes) — not a full signup yet, to
keep friction low for a cold prospect.
2. Form submission creates a tenant row (segment='CORPORATE_TMC', plan='FREE', sta-
tus='TRIAL') and routes an internal notification to the sales-motion owner (§9/§30) rather
than instantly self-provisioning — the human follow-up (a personalized sample report) hap-
pens before full account access, consistent with the founder-led-sales discipline in Part I
§9.
3. Once the prospect responds positively, an invite email is sent; clicking it lands on /signup
with the tenant pre-associated; the prospect sets a password or chooses magic-link (§28.1)
and becomes the tenant’s first TENANT_ADMIN.
4. On first login, the onboarding tour (§28.2) scoped to ANALYST/TENANT_ADMIN walks through
the Overview dashboard, highlighting the Elasticity chart specifically (since that’s the
artifact the sample report was built from) and prompting an upload for the full Overpay
Report (§29.6).
5. First dashboard load calls GET /api/v1/overview (§34.2), which the API gateway serves
from a short-TTL cache (§30.3) since this is very likely not the first request for that day’s
overview data.
63.2 A single collection cycle, minute by minute (illustrative, 06:30 IST cycle)
• 06:20–06:30: each source’s staggered random offset elapses; the scrape_cycle row
transitions PLANNED →RUNNING.
• 06:30: the sampling planner (§31.2/F044) generates SearchTask rows for every enabled
source × route × lead-bucket combination not already covered this cycle, pushed onto
the Redis Stream.
• 06:30–07:15: worker processes pull tasks, respecting per-source delay bounds and daily
caps (§33.4); each successful fetch writes a raw_capture row (hash, storage URI) and a
fare_quote_raw row; each failure is retried per the backoff policy (§31.2/F053) or marked
BLOCKED/FAILED.
• 07:15–07:20: the cleaner service consumes newly-written raw rows, runs them through
R01–R12 (§54), and writes fare_quote_clean rows or dq_issue quarantine entries.
• 07:20 onward (continuous): each accepted clean quote triggers the FLASH engine’s
atomic Lua update (§31.3), cascading cell →route →overall index values in Redis, pub-
lishing throttled IndexTick events the frontend can subscribe to.
• 07:25 (or once ≥70% of planned tasks resolve): the cycle transitions to COMPLETE
(or PARTIAL if the 3-hour deadline is approaching without full resolution); a progress event
is emitted to ops.cycle for the Data Health dashboard (§29.7).
• 23:30 (end of day, not this specific cycle): the OFFICIAL freeze (§31.3/F071) re-
computes the entire day from durable fare_quote_clean rows — independent of what-
ever the FLASH engine’s in-memory state happened to be — and, if checks pass, pub-
lishes apix_cell_daily/apix_series_daily rows with status='OFFICIAL', firing the
index.official.published webhook (§34.3) to any subscribed Enterprise tenant.

54. Full Cleaning Rule Catalogue (R01–R12) — Detailed
Each rule below runs in a fixed order inside the cleaning rule engine (§32.3, dq_rule/CleaningStage.apply())
returning Pass, Flag(code), or Reject(code). The first Reject short-circuits processing for
that quote; flags accumulate and are all recorded.
R01 — Schema validation. Confirms every required field (source, route, timestamp, at least
one price component) is present and correctly typed before anything else runs. Default ac-
tion: Reject. Why first: every downstream rule assumes a structurally valid record; running
price-logic rules on a malformed record would produce meaningless flags.
R02 — Currency check. Confirms the fare is denominated in INR; any other currency is logged
as a conversion event (relevant only if a future international-route extension, §53, is active) or
rejected if unexpected for a domestic-India source. Default action: Reject or flag depending
on source configuration.
R03 — Component identity check. Confirms total ≈base + fuel + taxes (+ con-
venience_fee) within a ₹1 tolerance. Default action: Flag and recompute the missing com-
ponent if only the total is known (feeding the tax-estimation logic in §32.3’s tax_rule table),
rather than rejecting outright — a missing breakdown is common and shouldn’t discard an
otherwise-valid price.
R04 — Absolute bounds check. Rejects any fare below ₹800 or above 10× the current route
median — a coarse, fast first-pass sanity filter that catches obvious scraping/parsing errors (a
fare of ₹18 or ₹18,00,000 on a domestic short-haul route) before the more computationally
expensive robust-outlier check runs.
R05 — Robust outlier detection (MAD-based). The system’s primary statistical defense:
computes a robust z-score against a trailing 14-day window of the same cell (route × lead
bucket × departure weekday), flagging scores above 5 and rejecting above 7 (both config-
urable) — see the full method in §32.3’s cell_stats design and the market-move protection
described alongside it, which prevents a genuine, broad price swing (e.g., a real festival-driven
spike affecting multiple carriers) from being mistaken for noise and erased.
R06 — Duplicate detection. Uses the dedup_hash (source, carrier, flight number, departure
timestamp, cabin, fare family, and a 15-minute-floored scrape time) to silently drop exact
repeat quotes, while flagging same-hash-different-price cases as CONFLICTING_DUPLICATE for
human review — the latter often signals either a genuine mid-window price change or a parsing
bug, and deserves a steward’s eyes rather than an automatic decision either way.
R07 — Cross-channel duplicate detection.
Identifies the same physical flight quoted
via both an airline-direct source and an OTA, and applies the configured channel policy
(DIRECT_FIRST by default, per §32.1/F062) rather than silently double-counting the same seat
in the index.
R08 — Sold-out handling. Excludes sold-out offers from price averages (as the SIH problem
statement explicitly requires) while still counting them toward a separate, useful sell-out-rate
metric surfaced in the Route drill-down dashboard (§29.3) — sold-out data is signal, not noise,
just not price signal.
R09 — Temporal consistency check. Rejects any quote with a departure date in the past
relative to its scrape timestamp, or with an internally inconsistent lead-time calculation (e.g., a
claimed L07 bucket that doesn’t match the actual days-to-departure) — a common symptom of
clock-handling bugs (timezone/midnight edge cases) that the system is specifically designed to
catch via IST-calendar-date lead-day computation (§“Lead-time window planner” in the source
AeroIndex specification).
R10 — Surge-jump detection. Flags a price jump exceeding 60% versus the same product
within a 2-hour window as SURGE_JUMP — usually a genuine, fast-moving demand event rather

AeroIndex / FareOS — Venture & Product Specification
76
than an error, and is routed to the anomaly-explanation engine (which cross-references the
event_calendar, §32.1) rather than being discarded.
R11 — Missing-components handling.
Records a quote with only a total price (no
base/fuel/tax breakdown) as valid for the headline total index but excluded from component
sub-indices (§“Alternate bases,” Part III) — this is the correct behavior because the total is still
real price information even when its breakdown is opaque.
R12 — Empty-result handling. Logs a search that returned zero results (a real “no data”

marked missing for that cycle rather than silently treated as a zero price — the missing-data

JSON, enabled flag) and are editable by a DATA_STEWARD with full audit logging, taking effect

behavior (surge thresholds, typical fare ranges) genuinely drifts over time, and a rule catalogue
that requires an engineering deploy to tune becomes stale.

33.4 Scraping-specific compliance and legal posture
This deserves explicit treatment because it is the area most likely to create real legal expo-
sure if handled carelessly, and it is treated as a governance function, not just an engineering

• Every source’s terms_reviewed record (who reviewed, when, outcome) gates whether

52. Regulatory & Ethics Deep Dive
52.1 Information Technology Act, 2000 (as amended) — relevance to data collection
India’s IT Act contains provisions relevant to unauthorized access to computer systems
(broadly, Section 43 and related provisions on unauthorized access/damage, and Section 66
on computer-related offences).
The operative risk for AeroIndex is not “scraping is illegal”
in the abstract — publicly accessible price information displayed to any visitor is materially
different from credential-protected data — but rather that specific behaviors (circumventing
access controls, solving CAPTCHAs to bypass an anti-bot measure, using another party’s
credentials) can shift a scraping approach from a legal gray area into clearer legal exposure.
This is precisely why the “Never” list in Part III §33.4 / F050 (never solve CAPTCHAs, never
spoof identities to evade blocks, never log in, never create bookings) is written as a hard engi-
neering constraint enforced in code, not a policy suggestion — it is the single most important
legal-risk-reduction decision in this entire specification, and it is treated as non-negotiable
regardless of any pressure to “just get the data.”
52.2 Contractual/ToS exposure
Each target site’s own Terms of Service may separately prohibit automated access, inde-
pendent of the IT Act.
The terms_reviewed gating mechanism (F043/F049, enforced in
the ref_source schema in §32.1) exists specifically so that a human — not a script — has
explicitly assessed and recorded a judgment (PERMITTED/DENIED/PENDING) for each source
before any live collection against it runs. The honest, stated position of this document:
a lightweight legal review (Part I §16, Stage 1 budget) should assess the specific ToS of each
target source before scaling live collection meaningfully beyond the single hackathon proof-of-
concept adapter, and the calibrated simulator (F046) remains the default data source for any
route/source combination where that review has not yet been completed or is unfavorable —
this is exactly why the simulator is architected as a permanent, not temporary, part of the
system (§31.2).
52.3 Reframing for the OTA/Airline commercial relationship
The single most effective risk-reduction move available is commercial, not technical: once an
OTA or airline becomes a paying data customer (Track C, Part I §9), the relationship inverts
from “we scrape your site” to “we sell you competitor intelligence you couldn’t ethically gather
about your own competitors any more easily than we can” — and many Track C conversations
can be structured so that the counterparty’s own fare data reaches AeroIndex via a licensed
feed or API rather than scraping, which is both cleaner legally and higher-quality data-wise.
This should be an explicit goal of every Track C sales conversation, not an afterthought.
52.4 Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDP), 2023
The system’s own data footprint is deliberately thin (§33.2): business-contact emails, organi-
zation metadata, and any voluntarily-uploaded booking data (for the Overpay Report, §29.6).
For that uploaded data specifically: - Purpose limitation is enforced at the product level — up-
loaded booking data is used only to generate the requested report and is not repurposed into
the core index or shared across tenants. - A data-processing agreement is signed with any
tenant uploading booking data, specifying retention period and deletion rights. - A self-serve
deletion path exists in /app/settings for any tenant to purge their uploaded data, logged in
the audit_log.

35. DevOps & Infrastructure
35.1 MVP architecture (hackathon →first paying customers)
• Single cloud region, containerized services (Docker Compose for local dev; a single small
Kubernetes cluster or even a managed container service for early production — deliber-

• Managed Postgres (Supabase/Neon/Railway free-to-low-cost tier), managed Redis (Up-

• Frontend on Vercel/Netlify (free tier initially).
• CI/CD: GitHub Actions — lint →type-check →unit tests →contract tests (adapter golden-
payload tests, per F043) →data-quality eval harness (make dq-eval, per F048, which fails
the build if recall on EXTREME_OUTLIERS or DUPLICATES drops below 0.9) →build →deploy
to staging →manual promote to production.
35.2 Scale architecture (post-seed, once enterprise/government SLAs require it)
• Split into the independently-scalable services described in §31.1’s service table, each with
its own deployment and autoscaling policy — the collector service scales on scrape-task
queue depth; the api-gateway/web-app scale on request volume.
• Move to a managed Kubernetes service (EKS/GKE/AKS) or equivalent once the number
of services and the need for fine-grained autoscaling/isolation outgrows a single small
cluster.
• Multi-AZ database deployment with automated failover; point-in-time recovery enabled.

56. Alternative Pricing & Business Models Considered — and Why They
Were Not Chosen as the Primary Path
Per the venture-architecture discipline of showing trade-offs rather than a single unexamined
“winner” (Part I §35), several alternative monetization paths were seriously considered before
settling on the tiered subscription/data-licensing model in Part I §10:
Pure consumer freemium (Hopper-style price-prediction app for Indian travelers).
Rejected as the primary near-term model — not because the category doesn’t monetize (§46.1
shows clearly that it can), but because it requires consumer-app-scale distribution spend and a
multi-year runway to reach the scale Hopper needed, in a market where ixigo and MakeMyTrip
already dominate consumer flight-search distribution. Retained as the free public-index tier
for credibility and SEO purposes, explicitly not as a revenue driver.
Pure government contract model (sell only to MoSPI/NSO/RBI). Rejected as the sole
model because government sales cycles and procurement rules (Part I §2.4, §6) make this too
slow and too small on its own to approach anything near ₹100cr in 30 months — retained as
the credibility anchor (Track A) but explicitly not modeled as the primary revenue line (Part I
§12).
Affiliate/commission model (earn a cut when a dashboard viewer books a flight
through a partner OTA). Considered as a possible consumer-monetization layer on top of
the free public index. Rejected for the 30-month window specifically because it requires the
kind of consumer traffic volume the free-tier distribution strategy (Part I §15) is not designed
to produce quickly, and because it would create a subtle conflict of interest with the “neutral,
independent index” positioning that is the entire credibility basis for the government and en-
terprise tracks — a visitor who suspects the index is influenced by which OTA pays the best
affiliate commission would reasonably trust it less. Not ruled out permanently, but explicitly
deferred past the 30-month window and past the point where the neutrality-based trust moat
(Part I §13) is firmly established.
Pure data-licensing-only model (sell raw+index data via API, no dashboards, no re-
ports, no enterprise sales motion — a “developer-first” API business). Considered
because it is the leanest possible model operationally. Rejected as the sole model because
the actual willingness-to-pay evidence in this market (per the buyer personas in §43) responds
much more strongly to a concrete, narrative deliverable (the Overpay Report, a dashboard a
non-technical travel manager can read) than to a raw API a developer would need to build tool-
ing around — the API remains a core deliverable (particularly for Track C), but is not sufficient
on its own to drive the Track B logo volume the Base Case model depends on.
Percentage-of-savings / performance-based pricing (charge a share of the demon-
strated travel-spend savings, rather than a flat subscription). Considered specifically
for Track B, since it aligns incentives cleanly and lowers the buyer’s perceived risk. Rejected
as the default model (though retained as an optional pilot-conversion lever, §9) because it re-
quires trustworthy, auditable measurement of “savings” that a skeptical finance team would
accept, which is operationally harder to stand up in the first 90 days than a simple flat annual
fee, and because it introduces revenue unpredictability that makes the financial model in Part

pricing lever once the Overpay Report’s savings-estimation methodology has enough validated

48. FAQ — Anticipated Questions from Judges, MoSPI, and Investors
“Isn’t this just a scraper with a chart on top?”
No — the differentiated engineering
is the data-quality harness (defect injection with measured precision/recall, F048), the
FLASH/OFFICIAL two-speed index engine with full auditability, and the DGCA-weighted, MoSPI-
methodology-aligned index construction — the scraping is deliberately the least novel part of
the system, and is treated that way in both the build priorities (§47.1) and the demo (§24).
“Why should MoSPI trust a privately-built index?” It shouldn’t, unconditionally — which
is why the ask (§44, Slide 14) is explicitly framed as a data-sharing pilot with a transparent,
back-tested methodology, not a request for endorsement. Trust is meant to be earned over
the 90-day pilot and beyond, not assumed from day one.
“What stops MakeMyTrip from just doing this themselves?” Structurally, their incen-
tives point away from it — publishing a neutral price index would arm both competitors and
regulators against their own dynamic-pricing practices, with no clear monetization upside for
them (§46.5).
This is a reasoned bet, not a guarantee — Part I §20 explicitly lists “a well-
capitalized incumbent enters the niche” as a real risk.
“How is this different from Google Flights’ price-history feature?” Google Flights is
a free, consumer-facing, ad/affiliate-monetized feature with thin publicly-documented India-
specific historical depth and no CPI-grade methodology transparency or institutional licensing
model — it is not a B2B/government data product and was never intended to be one.
“Can this really hit ₹100 crore in 30 months?”
Read honestly:
the pure fare-
index/benchmarking business plausibly reaches roughly ₹8–15 crore in a realistic Base
Case, and up to ~₹20–25 crore in an aggressive pure-data Upside Case — reaching closer to
₹100cr requires successfully expanding into a higher-ACV Corporate Travel Spend Intelligence
platform, funded by a seed round, with an aggressive (not guaranteed) enterprise sales
ramp (Part I §12). This document treats that outer case as the fundraising narrative, not the
operating plan, and says so explicitly rather than dressing it up.
“What’s the single biggest risk to this whole plan?”
That the founder-led enter-
prise/government sales motion (identified honestly as the weakest part of founder-market fit
in Part I §6) doesn’t convert fast enough to hit even the Base Case — which is exactly why
closing that specific capability gap (via a co-founder, advisor, or early hire) is called out as the
highest-leverage move in the first 12 months.
